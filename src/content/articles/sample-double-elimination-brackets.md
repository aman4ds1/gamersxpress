If you have watched a fighting game event or a major esports weekend, you have
seen a bracket split into two halves and heard commentators talk about
"lower bracket runs". This sample explains the structure in plain terms, so the
next bracket you read makes sense on the first pass.

## Two brackets, two lives

A double-elimination tournament gives every player or team two losses before
they are out. Winners stay in the **winners bracket**. The first loss drops
them into the **losers bracket**, where a second loss ends the run. The format
is described in detail on the
[Double-elimination tournament page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-elimination_tournament).

## How a typical run looks

1. Early rounds are often best-of-three, with later stages moving to
   best-of-five or longer.
2. A loss sends the loser sideways into the losers bracket, not home.
3. Players in the losers bracket play more matches to stay alive, which is why
   deep lower-bracket runs are physically longer days.
4. The winners of each half meet in the grand final.

## The grand final reset

The grand final has a built-in wrinkle. The winners-bracket finalist has not
lost a match, while the finalist arriving from the losers bracket already has
one loss. If the losers-bracket player wins the first set, the bracket is
"reset": both players have one loss, so a second set decides the tournament.
Commentators call the first set the reset condition and the decider the actual
final.

## Why organizers use it

Single elimination is faster and cheaper to run, but one bad match can eliminate
a favourite. Double elimination trades a longer schedule for a fairer result,
which is why it is standard for one-on-one fighting games and common across
[esports formats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esports) where production time
allows. When you read a bracket graphic, find the line separating the two
halves first, then follow whichever side a team sits on after their first loss.
